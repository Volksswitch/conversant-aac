Reviewing a conversation you have already had

A working sketch of something Conversant AAC does not do yet. Nothing here is real, nothing is saved anywhere except your own browser, and everything on the page can still change — which is the point of showing it to you now rather than later.

What we are asking you to look at

Conversant AAC listens to the person a user is talking with, and within a few seconds offers four things the user might say back. They pick one and the device speaks it. Every one of those conversations is already saved on the user's own device, in detail — and at the moment there is no way for anyone to look at one.

This is a sketch of that missing piece. The idea underneath it is that going back over a conversation is the only time the user can think about their own communication with nobody waiting on them. During a conversation everything is a race against the silence. Afterward there is no clock at all, so that is where the slow, careful thinking can happen: was that really what I wanted to say, and what would I rather have said instead?

Three things this deliberately is not. It never scores the user or tells them their conversation could have been better — remarks about the words are fine, verdicts on the person are not. It never changes anything without asking. And nothing here is required: every part of it can be skipped, and stopping loses nothing.

What would help most. Click through the four screens below and write down anything at all in the comment box under each one — including “this would never work” and “I do not understand what this is for”. Then answer as many of the seven questions at the bottom of the page as you have patience for. At the very bottom is a button that gathers everything you wrote into one block of text for you to copy into an email.

Nothing you type leaves this page. It is kept in your own browser so you can close the tab and come back, and it goes nowhere at all until you copy it and send it yourself.

Rehearsals are in the list too, and are marked. A rehearsal is in some ways the better thing to review: it can be run again, so “here is what I would rather have said” has somewhere to go, where a real conversation happened once. Nobody was let down by a poor reply either, which makes it easier to go back over. And it is the natural place for a clinician who wrote the scenario to sit down with the client.

Two things work differently there. There is no microphone in a rehearsal — the app speaks the other person's lines — so nothing can have been misheard, and that option is not offered. And the situation was made up, so a fact the user writes may have been invented to fit it: the app asks whether it is true of them or just part of the scenario, rather than assuming either way. How they want to sound, and a phrase for their buttons, carry over without a question. Is reviewing a rehearsal worth having, or would a client only ever want to look at real conversations?

The app knows which moments the user themselves asked for something different at the time — where they pressed for other suggestions, or gave up and typed instead — so it can say so on the row. It is a hint, not a filter: the app cannot know which moment actually mattered to them, and it should not pretend to.

Screen 2 of 4

Review happens on the conversation screen itself

This is not a separate part of the app. It is the screen the user already knows — the same four card positions, the same nine buttons along the middle, the same panel down the side — doing a different job. That matters more than it sounds: many of these users have a plastic keyguard over the screen with a hole cut for every button, and a review screen with its own layout would need its own keyguard.

Two of the three ways through turned out to be one. We had been treating “step through it one moment at a time” and “read the whole thing” as separate features. On this screen they are not: the whole conversation sits in the pane at the top with the exchange you are on outlined, so the two moment buttons move the outline and tapping any line moves it straight there. Scrolling to the part you remember and stepping through one at a time are the same screen.

So only one of the three is genuinely separate work: playing it back at the real speed, filling that same pane in time with a clock. It is the only way to feel how long the silences were, and the only one we would have to build on purpose. Is it worth it?

Screen 3 of 4 — the one we most need answered

Saying what you would rather have said

Here is the moment the user wants to talk about. The conversation is in the pane at the top with that exchange outlined; the four suggestions are below, exactly as they appeared. Tapping the card you would have wanted rewrites it in place — there is no separate writing box, because the sentence and the card are the same thing.

You leave review the way you leave a practice session — the Settings button. Review lives on a Settings tab beside Practice, so the button that took you in takes you back out, and it stays in the same place it occupies during a real conversation. It is the only one of the nine that means the same thing in both, so it is the only one that keeps its position. Nothing has to be saved first; what you write is kept as you write it.
The whole screen is one rule: tap the thing you want to say something about. Tap a line from another part of the conversation and you go there. Tap the other person's line on the exchange you are already on and you can correct the words the app wrote down. Tap a card and it becomes yours to change. Nothing else has to be learned, and there is no room on this screen for a question with three buttons under it anyway.

What a tap on a card means is read from what you do next. Tap the card you used and leave it alone — that one was right. Tap a different one — that one would have suited you better, which is worth knowing on its own, because under a four-second clock people take the first card that will do. Change the words — close, but here is how you would have put it. Press “my own words” — none of them were any use. Walk past without touching anything and nothing is recorded, which is how skipping a moment stays different from approving it.
One highlighted word is the cursor. A text cursor is a one-pixel line between two letters — hard to see and hard to hit. So instead there is always exactly one highlighted word: the two word buttons move it, tapping a word moves it straight there, typing replaces it, and backspace takes it out and leaves the gap. Three words is three presses of backspace.

We are not sure about the two icons — use the switch below to see both, and tell us whether either one says “a word at a time” without being explained. And we are not sure tapping a word is within reach for everyone, which is why the buttons exist as well and the tap is only ever a shortcut.

What comes of it

A review is only worth the effort if something changes. When the user writes what they would rather have said, the app offers to keep parts of it — and offers, never just does it. Adding a button moves every button after it on their panel, and those positions are learned by hand as much as by eye.

Where everything the user says in review would end up

What they say Where it goes
“This one was right”, or a sentence they wrote Kept as an example of how they want to sound, so suggestions gradually read more like them
A fact the app did not have About Me, the notes about that person, or the details of that place
A phrase they keep reaching for A button, for everyone or for one person
“It wrote down the wrong words” A note beside the record. Nothing for them to fix; it tells us where the listening struggles
“It was too slow” A setting they can change
“Nothing was wrong — I just wanted to say something else” Nothing is fixed. The sentence is still kept

That last row matters. Much of what somebody wishes they had said depended on something in their own head that morning. If every comment were pushed into the profile, the profile would fill up with things that were true for one afternoon and would shape every conversation afterward.
